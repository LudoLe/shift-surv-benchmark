\subsection{Computational cost}

Let $n$ be the number of patients per center, $K$ the number of labeled source
centers, $N = Kn$ the pooled source sample size, and $p$ the covariate
dimension. For the neural methods let $h$ denote the encoder width, $E$ the
number of epochs and $|\Lambda|$ the size of the hyperparameter grid searched by
importance-weighted cross-validation.

All compared methods are linear in the number of patients. Empirical risk
minimization, its importance-weighted variant, and the two invariance
objectives cost $\mathcal{O}(I N p)$ for $I$ optimizer iterations; the
importance weights add one auxiliary domain classifier of the same order over
$N + n$ points. CORAL additionally forms and whitens the two covariance
matrices, $\mathcal{O}(N p^{2} + p^{3})$, which is negligible here because
$p \ll n$. The post-hoc recalibrators are the cheapest: prior adjustment runs
expectation-maximization on scalar predicted probabilities,
$\mathcal{O}(J n)$ for $J$ iterations, and Platt and isotonic scaling fit on the
source validation split in $\mathcal{O}(n \log n)$. The adversarial methods are
the most expensive, $\mathcal{O}\!\left(|\Lambda| E N (ph + h^{2})\right)$,
where the grid factor $|\Lambda|$ comes from selecting the adversarial trade-off
and architecture without target labels.

Two points matter for the interpretation of our results. First, the cost
ordering does not track the performance ordering: the most expensive methods
(DANN, CDAN) do not outperform the cheapest baseline, so the absence of a
discrimination gain cannot be attributed to an insufficient compute budget.
Second, because every method scales linearly in $n$ and the covariate dimension
is small, the entire benchmark --- all shift regimes, methods and seeds --- runs
in a few CPU-hours, which keeps the study reproducible on commodity hardware.
